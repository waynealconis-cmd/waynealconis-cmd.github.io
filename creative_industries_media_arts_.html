Definitions, Domains, and Impact of the Creative Industries

This lesson explains what the creative industries are, where Media Arts belongs, how the major domains connect, and how creative work influences society and ordinary daily activities.

Learning Competency

Discuss the definitions and domains of the creative industries, with a focus on Media Arts, and explain their impact on society and daily life.

1. Creative Industries

Definition: The creative industries are fields in which imagination, artistic skill, cultural knowledge, technology, and intellectual property are used to create products, services, performances, messages, and experiences.

Examples: films, music, television programs, photographs, games, websites, advertisements, books, performances, visual designs, animation, and cultural products.

Why they are industries: Creative work involves not only making an artwork. It also includes planning, financing, production, promotion, distribution, exhibition, selling, licensing, and audience participation.

2. Media Arts

Definition: Media Arts is the creative use of communication media and technology to produce and share meaning. It may combine image, text, sound, movement, performance, design, code, and interaction.

Examples: a documentary, photo essay, animated video, podcast with visuals, school website, digital campaign, livestream, interactive story, mobile application, or multimedia performance.

Main purpose: Media Arts can inform, educate, entertain, persuade, document, preserve culture, promote a cause, or allow an audience to participate.

3. Creative Product, Service, and Experience

  • Product: an output people can view, use, purchase, or collect, such as a film, poster, photograph, game, or publication.
  • Service: creative work performed for a client, such as event coverage, branding, editing, advertising, or web design.
  • Experience: audience participation in a concert, exhibition, livestream, interactive website, virtual tour, or festival.

4. Creative Ecosystem

A creative ecosystem is the network of people and groups involved in turning an idea into an output and bringing it to an audience.

Stakeholders include: creators, writers, performers, designers, technicians, producers, teachers, clients, cultural groups, government agencies, schools, businesses, online platforms, distributors, and audiences.

How the Domains Work Together: One Example

Imagine a school campaign promoting environmental responsibility. A writer develops the message, a designer creates the visual identity, photographers and videographers document activities, editors prepare a short advocacy video, web developers build an interactive information page, performers present a school program, and social-media managers distribute the content. This shows that creative-industry domains are connected rather than completely separate.

Audiovisual Media: Definitions, Examples, and Explanations

Audiovisual Media communicates by coordinating moving images and sound over time. It includes film, television, documentary, animation, news features, advertisements, advocacy videos, educational videos, livestreams, and online short-form video.

Shot

A continuous recording made from the moment the camera starts until it stops.

Example: A wide shot records students entering the school gate.
Explanation: Every shot selects what the audience can see, from what position, and for how long.

Scene

A group of shots taking place in one location or continuous period of story time.

Example: A classroom scene includes a wide shot, teacher medium shot, student close-ups, and detail shots.
Explanation: Editors combine related shots to build a complete event, idea, or dramatic unit.

Framing and Composition

The placement of subjects and visual elements inside the image boundary.

Example: A student is placed on a rule-of-thirds point with look room in the direction of attention.
Explanation: Composition controls emphasis, balance, depth, relationships, and the information included or excluded.

Montage

A sequence that combines separate images and sounds to compress time or build an idea.

Example: Short shots show collecting materials, cleaning a site, planting, and the completed garden.
Explanation: Meaning comes from the relationship between shots, not only from each shot separately.

Diegetic Sound

Sound understood as coming from within the story world.

Example: Dialogue, footsteps, a radio heard by a character, and classroom ambience.
Explanation: Diegetic sound establishes believable space and may provide story information.

Non-diegetic Sound

Sound added for the audience but not heard by the characters.

Example: Background score, an explanatory narrator, or a dramatic audio sting.
Explanation: It can organize information and shape interpretation, but should not overpower speech or manipulate factual meaning.

Continuity Editing

Editing that makes screen time, space, and action feel clear and connected.

Example: A learner reaches for a door in one shot and completes the action from another angle.
Explanation: Matching action, eye lines, screen direction, sound, props, and lighting prevents confusing jumps.

B-roll

Supporting footage shown over interviews, narration, or a main sequence.

Example: While a craftsperson explains weaving, the audience sees hands, tools, fibers, patterns, and the finished product.
Explanation: B-roll supplies visual evidence, illustrates details, hides necessary edits, and improves pacing.

Lesson 1

Audiovisual Language and Forms

What is audiovisual media?

Audiovisual media combines moving images and sound. Meaning is created not only by what is shown, but also by framing, sequence, duration, voice, music, silence, and the relationship between shots.

Common SHS forms

  • Narrative short: fictional story with characters and conflict
  • Documentary: evidence-based treatment of real people, places, or issues
  • News feature: factual and audience-focused reporting
  • Advertisement or advocacy video: persuasive message with a call to action
  • Music or experimental video: rhythm, mood, visual metaphor, and creative form
  • Educational video: explains a process or concept clearly
Analyze

Choose a one-minute video. Identify its purpose, intended audience, dominant shots, important sounds, and message. Explain how one production choice affects meaning.

Composition and movement

  • Use the rule of thirds, headroom, look room, leading lines, balance, and depth.
  • Keep movement motivated. Pan follows horizontally, tilt moves vertically, and tracking moves with the subject.
  • Record each shot longer than needed and capture wide, medium, close-up, and detail coverage.
  • Maintain continuity of screen direction, action, costume, props, and lighting.

Lighting with available resources

  • Key light: main source shaping the subject
  • Fill: softens strong shadows
  • Back light: separates subject from background
  • Window light and white illustration board can work as low-cost tools.
  • Avoid unsafe electrical setups, blocked exits, glare, and filming directly into intense light.

Viewing guide: note frame rate, shutter speed, camera movement, and audio. After viewing, demonstrate three stable shots using a phone.

Lesson 4

Sound: The Other Half of the Story

Sound layers

  • Dialogue or interview: primary spoken information
  • Narration: guides context and structure
  • Ambient sound: establishes the real environment
  • Sound effects: emphasizes actions or transitions
  • Music: supports pace and tone without overpowering speech
  • Silence: can create focus, pause, or contrast

Clean-audio routine

  1. Choose the quietest suitable location.
  2. Place the microphone near the speaker but outside the frame.
  3. Wear headphones and monitor before recording.
  4. Record 30 seconds of room tone.
  5. Check for clipping, wind, handling noise, and echo.
  6. Use only music and effects you created or are permitted to use, then give credit as required.

Sound-only challenge

In groups, record a 30-second soundscape of a school environment. Arrange at least four original sounds to suggest a place and activity without showing an image. Classmates infer the setting and explain the evidence they heard.

Lesson 5

Editing, Feedback, and Responsible Publishing

Post-production workflow

  1. Back up and label files by scene, shot, and take.
  2. Review footage and select technically usable, meaningful clips.
  3. Create a rough cut focused on story and sequence.
  4. Refine pacing, continuity, transitions, and B-roll.
  5. Mix speech, ambience, effects, and music.
  6. Correct exposure and color consistently.
  7. Add readable titles, captions, credits, and source acknowledgments.
  8. Export, quality-check on another device, and publish for the intended audience.

Editing principles

  • Cut for meaning, not because an effect is available.
  • Use transitions sparingly. A straight cut is often clearest.
  • Let B-roll support what the audience hears.
  • Do not manipulate interviews to change the speaker’s intended meaning.
  • Use captions and clear contrast to improve accessibility.
  • Check copyright, privacy, consent, names, and credits before uploading.

Digital Interactive Media: Definitions, Examples, and Explanations

Digital Interactive Media combines text, graphics, animation, audio, video, data, and computer programming in an experience that changes when a user clicks, taps, types, moves, chooses, or gives another input.

Interactivity

A two-way relationship in which a digital product receives user input and produces a response.

Example: A quiz displays feedback after a learner selects an answer.
Explanation: The learner is not only watching. The learner influences what happens next.

User Interface or UI

The visible controls and screen elements through which a person uses a product.

Example: Menus, navigation bars, icons, buttons, sliders, forms, and game controls.
Explanation: A clear interface helps users recognize available actions without needing lengthy instructions.

User Experience or UX

The overall quality of a person’s experience before, during, and after using an interactive product.

Example: A school website loads quickly, works on a phone, uses readable text, and lets students find schedules easily.
Explanation: UX includes usefulness, usability, accessibility, trust, and satisfaction, not appearance alone.

Prototype

An early model used to show, test, and improve an idea before completing the final product.

Example: A paper wireframe or linked slide presentation representing an app.
Explanation: Testing a prototype reveals confusing navigation and missing information while changes are still inexpensive.

Responsive Design

A design approach that adapts content and layout to different screen sizes.

Example: A three-column computer layout becomes a single column on a phone.
Explanation: Responsive design improves readability and access across phones, tablets, and computers.

Accessibility

Designing so people with different abilities and ways of using technology can access the content.

Example: Alternative text, captions, sufficient color contrast, keyboard controls, and clear labels.
Explanation: Accessible design provides more learners with an equivalent opportunity to understand and interact.

Design: Definitions, Examples, and Explanations

Design is a purposeful process of understanding a need, generating ideas, organizing visual or physical elements, making prototypes, and improving a solution for a particular user, audience, and context.

Visual Hierarchy

The arrangement of elements to show which information should be noticed first, second, and next.

Example: A poster uses the event title as the largest text, followed by date and venue, then contact details.
Explanation: Scale, contrast, color, position, spacing, and type weight guide the viewer’s attention.

Typography

The purposeful selection and arrangement of letters and text.

Example: A readable sans serif typeface is used for small screen text while a display face is used for a short heading.
Explanation: Typography affects readability, tone, organization, and accessibility.

Color Scheme

A planned set of colors used to support meaning, mood, recognition, and contrast.

Example: Blue and green support an environmental campaign, with a dark text color for readable contrast.
Explanation: Color should serve the communication goal and remain understandable for users with color-vision differences.

Grid and Alignment

A hidden structure used to organize text and images consistently.

Example: Poster information aligns to columns and common edges.
Explanation: Alignment creates relationships between elements and makes complex information easier to scan.

Iteration

The cycle of making, testing, receiving feedback, and revising.

Example: Students produce three poster drafts, test them with classmates, and improve the strongest option.
Explanation: Professional design rarely appears in one attempt. Revision uses evidence to make the solution clearer and more useful.

Design Rationale

A concise explanation of how design decisions respond to the brief.

Example: The designer explains why a large bilingual headline and familiar local image fit the intended audience.
Explanation: A rationale connects choices to purpose, audience, research, and criteria rather than personal taste alone.

Performing Arts: Definitions, Examples, and Explanations

Performing Arts are artistic forms presented through live or recorded action, including theater, dance, music, spoken word, puppetry, and interdisciplinary or multimedia performance.

Performance Space

The physical or digital environment in which performers and audiences encounter a work.

Example: A classroom in the round, an auditorium stage, a community plaza, or a livestream frame.
Explanation: Space affects visibility, movement, sound, audience relationship, safety, and production design.

Blocking

The planned positions and movements of performers within the performance space.

Example: An actor crosses downstage during an important line while the ensemble forms a still image behind.
Explanation: Blocking makes action readable, supports meaning, balances the stage picture, and prevents unsafe collisions.

Devising

A collaborative process of creating performance through research, improvisation, selection, composition, and revision.

Example: A group creates a short piece from interviews about student commuting experiences.
Explanation: The script or score develops from exploration rather than being completely fixed at the beginning.

Cue

A signal that triggers an action in performance or technical production.

Example: A spoken line triggers a sound effect and a change in lighting.
Explanation: Cue sheets coordinate performers, stage management, audio, lighting, video, projection, and transitions.

Ensemble

A group whose members listen, respond, support one another, and work toward a unified performance.

Example: A dance group maintains shared timing while allowing individual roles.
Explanation: Ensemble quality depends on preparation, trust, awareness, discipline, and responsibility.

Documentation

The planned recording and description of both process and final performance.

Example: A rehearsal journal, photographs, program, cue sheet, and edited performance video.
Explanation: Documentation supports assessment, reflection, promotion, archiving, and portfolio development.

Visual Arts: Definitions, Examples, and Explanations

Visual Arts are creative practices that communicate mainly through visible form. They include drawing, painting, printmaking, sculpture, photography, digital illustration, installation, collage, mixed media, and video art.

Medium

The material or technological form used to make an artwork.

Example: Charcoal on paper, acrylic paint, digital photography, recycled-object assemblage, or vector illustration.
Explanation: Each medium offers different possibilities for mark, texture, scale, color, reproduction, cost, and durability.

Technique

The learned method used to control or explore a medium.

Example: Cross-hatching in drawing, layering in paint, long exposure in photography, or masking in digital art.
Explanation: Technique supports meaning when selected intentionally rather than used only to display skill.

Composition

The arrangement of visual elements within an artwork or image.

Example: A photographer places the main subject off-center and uses a road as a leading line.
Explanation: Composition directs attention, creates balance or tension, controls visual movement, and frames relationships.

Subject Matter and Theme

Subject matter is what is shown; theme is the broader idea being explored.

Example: A painted river is the subject, while environmental responsibility may be the theme.
Explanation: Separating the two helps students explain meaning beyond naming visible objects.

Artist Statement

A short text explaining the work’s idea, context, process, medium, and intended audience experience.

Example: A student explains how family photographs and woven textures explore memory and migration.
Explanation: An artist statement supports interpretation without replacing the visual evidence in the work.

Curation

The purposeful selection, grouping, sequencing, labeling, and presentation of artworks.

Example: A digital exhibition arranges five works from observation to imagined future.
Explanation: Curation creates relationships among works and shapes how an audience encounters the exhibition.

Guided Discussion for Senior High School

Use these discussion points before, during, or after the lessons. Ask students to support answers with examples, observations, research, and evidence from their creative process.

Looking before interpreting

Teach students to describe visible evidence before giving interpretations. Then connect elements, principles, medium, technique, subject, context, function, and audience to a supported reading.

Idea, medium, and technique

Discuss how a selected medium changes what can be expressed. Encourage experimentation with traditional, found, photographic, digital, and mixed media while documenting results and following safety procedures.

Originality and reference

Students may learn from artworks and cultural sources, but should transform ideas, identify references, credit creators, respect intellectual property, and avoid presenting copied work as original.

Presenting and curating

Discuss how selection, sequence, spacing, lighting, labels, captions, mounting, digital display, accessibility, and audience movement affect meaning. A portfolio should include both outcomes and evidence of process.

Discussion questions

  • What is the purpose, intended audience, and context of the work?
  • Which elements, techniques, tools, or processes are most important, and why?
  • What specific example demonstrates the concept clearly?
  • How could the work affect society, culture, livelihood, education, or daily life?
  • What legal, ethical, safety, accessibility, or cultural responsibilities apply?
  • What would you revise after audience feedback, and what evidence supports that revision?
